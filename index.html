Model risk — the flagship

Scoring trades on whether they survive — not just where they land

Most tools score an options structure on the terminal probability of profit: the chance the stock finishes, at expiry, in a profitable range. For a position you actively manage and exit early, that overstates the edge — a break-even touched mid-life stops you out even if price would have finished back inside the zone. So I score expected value off the path probability instead: a first-passage (barrier-hit) probability of holding to target without ever breaching a break-even, computed from the reflection principle under a lognormal diffusion.

The closed form is exact for each single barrier but combines the two break-evens additively, which omits the joint-crossing term — a known approximation. The interesting question isn't whether it's biased, but by how much, and against what benchmark. A naive Monte-Carlo that only checks the barrier at discrete steps misses crossings between steps, so it overstates survival — it is itself biased. Benchmarked against a Brownian-bridge continuity-corrected Monte-Carlo of the same diffusion (the honest benchmark), the closed form is near-exact for typical candidates — median error ≈ 0.004, at the Monte-Carlo noise floor — and materially conservative only when both break-evens are close. What makes the residual bias real rather than noise is its direction: it understates survival in 25 of 30 tests, never optimistic.

Stated up front This is a screening / ranking estimator, not a calibrated absolute probability. A near-perfect rank correlation is what drives candidate selection; the small residual bias is conservative — it can only push a marginal trade below the acceptance threshold, never over-select a bad one. For a use needing the absolute probability, the principled fix is a two-boundary (double-barrier) first-passage treatment, not a one-line correction.

The research core is open-source — CI-tested, zero third-party dependencies, no vendor data. Reproduce this figure with python examples/first_passage_vs_montecarlo.py.

How I work — the model-risk process

Independent challenge, before acceptance

I built and ran the options research under a multi-agent process. An implementer proposed models and wrote the code; an independent challenger's only job was to falsify each result under pre-registered criteria; I owned every accept-or-reject decision. Nothing closed without a disconfirming test on the record — the discipline that caught, characterized, and constrained a first-passage model's bias before it was ever trusted. The agents drew on a curated corpus of finance, quantitative-methods, and computer-science books and articles.
